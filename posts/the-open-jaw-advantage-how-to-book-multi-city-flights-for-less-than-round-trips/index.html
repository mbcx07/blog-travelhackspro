The Open-Jaw Advantage: How to Book Multi-City Flights for Less Than Round-Trips

2026-07-23 · Flights · Tags: The, flights

Most travelers default to the same booking pattern: search for a round-trip flight, pick the cheapest dates, and click buy. But that habit is costing you hundreds of dollars—and missing out on the chance to see two cities for the price of one. The secret lies in open-jaw and multi-city itineraries, a booking strategy that savvy travelers use to unlock savings, add flexibility, and even turn a simple trip into a mini-adventure. In this guide, I’ll show you exactly how to master this technique, when it works in your favor, and how to avoid the pitfalls that can turn a clever hack into a costly mistake.

What Exactly Is an Open-Jaw Itinerary?

An open-jaw flight is one where you fly into one city and out of another, without backtracking to your original arrival point. For example, instead of flying New York to London and back to New York, you fly New York to London, then later fly from Paris back to New York. The “open jaw” is the gap between London and Paris—you’re responsible for getting between those two cities, but the airline only prices the two one-way segments. This is different from a multi-city itinerary, which can include multiple stops and even different airlines, but the core principle is the same: you’re not locked into a single round-trip.

Why does this matter? Because airlines often price round-trips based on the assumption that you’ll return from the same city. When you fly into one city and out of another, you’re effectively creating two one-way tickets, which can sometimes be cheaper than a traditional round-trip—especially if you’re traveling to a region with multiple hubs. For instance, a round-trip from Chicago to Rome might cost $1,200, but an open-jaw from Chicago to Rome and then from Florence back to Chicago could be $850, because the airline’s fare rules treat the segments differently. The savings come from avoiding the “backtrack” that airlines build into round-trip pricing.

When Open-Jaw Saves You the Most Money

Open-jaw itineraries shine in specific scenarios. The first is when you’re visiting a country or region with multiple major airports. Think of a trip to Japan: you could fly into Tokyo and out of Osaka, saving you the cost and hassle of a domestic flight or a long train ride back. Similarly, in Europe, flying into London and out of Paris is a classic open-jaw that lets you travel overland between the two cities without paying for a separate flight. The key is to look for routes where the airline has a strong presence at both endpoints—this often means lower fares because the airline can fill seats on both legs.

Another sweet spot is when you’re traveling during peak season. Round-trip fares to popular destinations spike in summer, but open-jaw fares can sometimes be less affected because they’re priced as separate one-ways. For example, a round-trip from Los Angeles to Cancún in December might be $600, but an open-jaw into Cancún and out of Mexico City could be $450, even though you’re covering more ground. The airline sees the second leg as a less popular route, so it prices it lower. This is especially true for destinations with multiple airports that serve different tourist flows.

Finally, open-jaw is a lifesaver for multi-destination trips. If you’re planning a two-week vacation that includes both a beach and a city, you don’t want to waste a day traveling back to your original airport. An open-jaw lets you start in one place and end in another, maximizing your time and minimizing backtracking. The savings aren’t just monetary—they’re also in the form of extra hours of vacation you’d otherwise spend on a train or a short-haul flight.

How to Use Multi-City Search Tools Like a Pro

Most booking sites have a “multi-city” option, but few travelers use it because they assume it’s more expensive. In reality, it’s your best friend for finding open-jaw deals. Google Flights is the gold standard here. When you click “Multi-city,” you can enter your outbound and return segments separately, and the tool will show you the combined price. The trick is to experiment with different city pairs. For example, if you’re flying from New York to Europe, try entering New York to London, then Paris to New York. But also try New York to London, then Amsterdam to New York—you might be surprised that the latter is cheaper, even though Amsterdam is farther from London than Paris.

Another powerful feature is the “Explore” map on Google Flights. You can set your origin and dates, then zoom out to see prices to various destinations. But to use it for open-jaw, you need to think in terms of “in” and “out” airports. A better approach is to use the multi-city search with flexible dates. For instance, if you’re flexible on when you leave and return, you can use the calendar view to see the cheapest days for each segment. Often, the cheapest outbound day is different from the cheapest return day, and combining them can yield a lower total than a fixed round-trip.

Other tools like Kayak and Skyscanner also have multi-city options, but they’re less intuitive. My advice: start with Google Flights, then cross-check with the airline’s own website. Sometimes airlines offer exclusive deals on multi-city itineraries that aren’t available on aggregators. For example, if you’re flying on a single airline for both segments, you might be able to book a “stopover” fare that includes a free night in the connecting city—more on that later.

The Stopover Hack: Turning a Layover into a Free Mini-Vacation

Open-jaw and multi-city aren’t just about saving money—they can also give you a free stopover. Many airlines, especially those with hub-and-spoke models, allow you to add a stopover at no extra cost, or for a small fee, when you book a long-haul flight. This is a game-changer for travelers who want to see two destinations on one ticket. For example, Icelandair offers free stopovers in Reykjavik on flights between North America and Europe. You can fly from New York to London, but with a stopover in Iceland for a few days, all for the same price as a direct flight. Similarly, Turkish Airlines offers stopovers in Istanbul, and TAP Air Portugal offers them in Lisbon.

To take advantage of this, you need to book a multi-city itinerary that includes the stopover as a separate segment. For instance, instead of booking New York to London direct, you book New York to Reykjavik, then Reykjavik to London, with a few days in between. The airline’s fare rules often allow this without a price increase, especially if you’re flying on a single ticket. The key is to search for “stopover” or “free stopover” on the airline’s website, or use a tool like Google Flights and look for itineraries with long layovers. Some airlines even have dedicated stopover programs that include hotel discounts and tours.

But here’s the pro tip: you don’t have to limit yourself to official stopover programs. You can create your own stopover by booking an open-jaw that includes a layover in a city you want to explore. For example, if you’re flying from San Francisco to Bangkok, you might find a flight that connects through Seoul. Instead of taking the shortest connection, you could intentionally book a longer layover—say, 24 hours—and explore Seoul. Many airlines allow this at no extra cost, as long as the layover is within the allowed maximum. You just need to search for flights with longer connection times, or use the multi-city tool to add a separate segment. This turns a mundane layover into a bonus destination.

Combining One-Way Fares: The Underrated Alternative

Sometimes the simplest open-jaw is just booking two one-way tickets. This is especially effective when you’re flying on low-cost carriers or when you’re mixing airlines. For example, you might fly from New York to London on a budget airline like Norse Atlantic, then return from Paris on a different airline like French Bee. Because these airlines don’t have round-trip pricing, booking two one-ways is often cheaper than any round-trip option. Even on legacy carriers, two one-ways can be cheaper if you’re flexible with dates and airports. The trick is to search for each leg separately, then compare the total to a round-trip.

One caution: booking two one-ways means you’re not protected if the first flight is delayed and you miss the second. But if you’re booking with a buffer—say, a day or two between flights—it’s a low-risk strategy. Also, be aware that some airlines charge more for one-way tickets, especially on international routes. But with the rise of low-cost long-haul carriers, this is changing. For example, you can often find one-way transatlantic fares for under $200, which is less than half of a typical round-trip. If you’re planning a multi-city trip, this can be a huge win.

Another advantage of one-ways is that you can use different loyalty programs. For instance, you might book the outbound on United using miles, and the return on American using a credit card travel portal. This lets you maximize your points and avoid the hassle of trying to find award availability on a single airline for a round-trip. It also gives you more flexibility to change plans without paying a hefty change fee, since each ticket is independent.

Advanced Tactics: Using Alliances and Codeshares to Your Advantage

If you’re a frequent flyer, open-jaw itineraries can be even more powerful when you leverage airline alliances. For example, if you’re flying from the U.S. to Asia, you might book a flight on United (Star Alliance) into Tokyo, then a separate flight on ANA (also Star Alliance) out of Osaka. Because they’re in the same alliance, you can often book this as a single ticket, which means your baggage will be checked through and you’ll earn miles on both segments. But the real hack is using award miles. Many loyalty programs allow open-jaw awards at the same price as a round-trip, as long as the total distance is within the program’s rules. For instance, on United’s MileagePlus, you can book an open-jaw award for the same miles as a round-trip, as long as the two destinations are in the same region. This is a fantastic way to see two cities on points without paying extra.

Another advanced tactic is to use codeshare flights to create an open-jaw that isn’t obvious. For example, you might book a flight on Delta (SkyTeam) from New York to Amsterdam, then a codeshare flight on KLM from Brussels back to New York. Even though the second flight is operated by KLM, it’s booked under Delta’s code, so it counts as a single ticket. This can sometimes unlock lower fares because the airline’s pricing system sees it as a round-trip to the same region, even though you’re flying into different cities. To find these, you need to search on the airline’s website and use the “multi-city” option, then try different combinations of airports. It takes a bit of trial and error, but the savings can be substantial.

Finally, don’t forget about budget airline alliances. For example, the U-Fly Alliance includes carriers like Hong Kong Express and Lucky Air, and they offer interline connections that can be booked as a single itinerary. While these are less common, they can be a goldmine for open-jaw deals in Asia. The key is to always search for multi-city options, even if you think you’re booking a simple round-trip. You might be surprised at what you find.

Conclusion

The open-jaw advantage is one of the most underutilized tools in a traveler’s arsenal. By breaking free from the round-trip mindset, you can save money, see more places, and add flexibility to your itinerary. Whether you’re booking a simple two-city trip or a complex multi-stop adventure, the strategies outlined here—using multi-city search tools, exploiting stopover programs, combining one-way fares, and leveraging alliances—will help you travel smarter. The next time you plan a trip, don’t just search for a round-trip. Spend a few extra minutes experimenting with different city pairs and dates. You might just unlock a deal that pays for your next vacation. Happy travels, and remember: the best fare is the one you never knew existed.
